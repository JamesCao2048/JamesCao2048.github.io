## Why employers still care about your education

I haven't written much lately. I've been busy helping a few friends look for work, mostly in software development and product roles in China's tech industry. From what I've seen, this year's job search has been brutal. The large companies seem to be advertising far fewer positions than last year. People who would previously have had several offers are struggling to get even a handful of interviews.

The impact of AI agents feels hard to ignore. I'm glad I entered the industry when I did. If I were starting now, I'd probably be questioning myself too.

Even the discussion around artificial general intelligence, or AGI, has shifted toward claims that it is already here. In NVIDIA's August earnings call, Jensen Huang [suggested that this was already true for many tasks](https://investor.nvidia.com/files/content_files/TRANSCRIPT_-NVIDIA-Corp-NVDA-US-Q2-2027-Earnings-Call-26-August-2026-5_00-PM-ET.pdf). People approaching retirement have assets and networks. Those of us already working have experience and a position in the industry. **What are people just graduating supposed to do to find a good job?**

I've been thinking about this for a year or two. My original answer was fairly simple: learn to use AI better than the people around you, use it to learn and work much faster, and build worthwhile projects that demonstrate your ability. Surely good offers would follow.

A friend pointed out the problem. A recruiter may screen you out because of your degree or where you studied, before you ever get an interview. Your projects never get a chance to speak for you.

I still think employers will have less reason to rely so heavily on educational background over time. In my experience, once someone has worked for three to five years, employers pay more attention to their projects and results. After ten years, their educational background seems to matter very little.

So why does educational background still matter so much when companies hire new graduates?

I think a large part of the answer is that new graduates rarely have enough substantial work to show. Employers need a way to assess people who have little work experience. Their degrees, where they studied, their internships, and how many papers they have published are relatively easy to compare.

## AI may help people become better engineers, not just work faster

AI could change this. Becoming a senior engineer has usually meant spending several years learning on the job. Someone who takes initiative, knows what they want to do, and uses AI well may now learn to handle that work much sooner. That willingness to choose a direction and act on it is what I mean by agency.

Some of the examples are striking. [Xinhua Finance reported on Guangyu Chen](https://m.cnfin.com/gs-lb/zixun/20260727/4446573_1.html), a recent high-school graduate contributing to work behind Kimi K3; he is also listed among the authors of the [Kimi K3 technical report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24653). Young researchers and founders, sometimes only just out of university, make me question how much age or years of experience can tell us about what someone is capable of.

By _senior_, I mean more than being able to write code or complete assigned tasks. I mean recognizing which problems are worth solving for the business, deciding what to work on first, getting people to work together, finding the resources needed, and seeing the work through. Doing that well helps an engineer build influence and take on more important problems.

These skills have usually taken years to learn on the job. What interests me is that **AI may help people learn to make these decisions, as well as get their work done faster.**

My own experience is part of why I think so. My department gives people considerable freedom. Our managers set a broad direction, but leave us room to decide what to work on and how to approach it. Almost as soon as I joined, I had to work out where I could contribute something valuable.

At first I was lost. I didn't know what would be worth doing, or how to get started. Agents were less capable then. I thought things through on my own, talked to people, read code line by line, and debugged one issue after another. It took months before I had something to show for all that work.

As agents became more capable this year, I began asking them to help me think about which problems mattered and which should come first. I was using AI as a kind of career coach. Its answers weren't necessarily right, but they gave me ideas I hadn't considered and helped me identify the important problems and get started sooner.

I've also noticed that some people with years of experience still focus mainly on the tasks they are given. They sometimes wonder whether what they're doing matters, but struggle to work out what to do about it.

If the work seems unimportant, could they find a problem more worth their time? Could they discuss a different direction with their manager? Could they make their existing work more thorough or systematic, so that it becomes more useful? They may already be asking themselves these questions without knowing how to act on them.

I don't know whether the difference is about taking initiative, years of habit, or the way their workplace operates. But AI seems to help different people in very different ways. Some use it mainly to complete their assignments faster. Others also use it to decide what deserves their effort, how to approach it, and what they need to learn.

That is why I think a young person who takes initiative could learn to handle a senior engineer's responsibilities sooner than before, using AI to try out ideas and think through their choices.

At the same time, I expect companies to need fewer traditional junior roles as agents take on more of the tasks juniors used to do. They may increasingly prefer people who can take a task from start to finish on their own. My concern is that, while a junior engineer is still learning, an agent may cost less than hiring and training them—and that the cost gap could widen.

If that happens, graduate hiring could become a luxury that only a small number of large companies can afford, with fewer places available. That is what I worry could happen; the job searches I've seen cannot establish that it will.

The familiar path—graduate, join a company as a junior, learn for several years, become a senior—would then become less dependable. More students might have to prepare before graduation for work that used to require several years of experience. That is a difficult prospect.

For students, the most useful response I can think of is to start working on **real projects that help them learn and show what they can do** as early as possible. Those projects might come from internships, university work, a training program, or an idea someone cares enough about to pursue independently with AI.

It also makes me wonder whether computer science education should shift more of its emphasis from lectures and conventional assignments toward open-ended projects. I don't mean another operating-systems assignment with a known answer. My concern is that AI can already complete many such assignments from end to end.

I mean projects where students must discover a problem, judge whether someone needs a solution, design an approach, test it, and change course. Ideally, the result should be useful to someone. If a student has completed several valuable projects of that kind before graduating, why shouldn't they apply for roles that previously required years of experience?

## Hiring teams cannot examine every project in depth

If I were hiring, I would want to judge people by what they can actually do. An open-source project with many GitHub stars, or substantial research in a technical area, could tell me more than a degree.

But projects and research contributions can be exaggerated or misrepresented too. To assess them properly, I'd need to inspect the code, understand the person's contribution, perhaps look at how they worked with AI, and ask detailed questions in an interview.

If a role attracts a thousand résumés, how could a hiring team do that for every applicant?

This was the constraint I initially overlooked: **employers need to find capable people without spending too much time and money assessing every applicant.** Degrees, LeetCode-style coding interviews, and questions with stock answers all have limitations. In China, candidates often call the last category _baguwen_: standardized technical questions whose answers can be memorized. One reason employers keep using them may be that they have so many applicants to assess.

AI may help some young people become capable engineers much sooner. What they can do may have less and less to do with their educational background, age, or years on the job. But **people may get better at their work faster than employers get better at recognizing their abilities.** Hiring costs, assessment practices, and organizational habits can change much more slowly than the tools people use to learn.

So I expect employers to keep using degrees and educational background to screen applicants for now. I still believe these will matter less over time, if employers find better ways to assess what candidates can actually do.

That leaves a question I'd like hiring teams to take seriously: how could AI help them evaluate a candidate's real projects, technical judgment, and problem-solving process at a reasonable cost, so they rely less on degrees and years of experience?
